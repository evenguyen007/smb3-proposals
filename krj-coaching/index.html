Stop trying to "stand up straight." Here's what neutral actually looks like in a hypermobile body.

Slide 1 · Cover
"Stand up straight" is the worst cue you can give a hypermobile body.
Visual: Soft cream backdrop. Side-profile silhouette of someone over-correcting — ribs flared, chin poking, knees locked. Caption overlay in your handwriting font.
Slide 2 · The trap
Most of us heard it as kids.
Shoulders back. Don't slouch. Stand up straight. For a body with end-range joints, that cue creates a brand new pattern — shoulders squeeze back, chest pushes forward, ribs lift, and now the whole system is bracing on top of an unstable base. ❤️
Slide 3 · What changes
Neutral isn't rest.
Neutral is the supported playground where nothing is overworking or collapsing. It's not effort-free — it's effort that's distributed. That's a different skill, and most hypermobile people have never been taught it.
Slide 4 · The reset (1 of 5)
Feet under hips. Wider than you think.
A narrow base = a wobbly foundation. Spread the feet. Spread the toes. The big toe joint, the little-toe-side kickstand, and the heel — three contact points to start with.
Slide 5 · The reset (2 of 5)
Soften the knees. Don't lock them.
Locked knees feel "stable" because the joint is hanging on the ligament. Soft knees feel destabilizing at first — that's the work. That's where strength and joint safety live.
Slide 6 · The reset (3 of 5)
Pelvic floor flashlight pointing down.
Between the arches and heels. Ribs long. Stack the ribcage over the pelvis. ✨
Slide 7 · The reset (4 of 5)
Breathe into the back of the ribcage.
The bra-line zone. The sneaky tight spot for bendy bodies. 360-degree expansion, not chest-up.
Slide 8 · The reset (5 of 5)
Tear the floor apart. 5 to 10% effort.
Light side-hip activation. Wakes up the support team that "stand up straight" has been hiding from you for years.

Highly capable in some areas, completely overwhelmed in others. Years of wondering why my brain kept pushing while my body felt stressed. Several full cycles of burnout — frikking hard, sometimes devastating.
Slide 3 · The connection
Then I learned: hypermobility and ADHD overlap more often than chance.
Both involve differences in nervous system processing — proprioception, sensory filtering, regulation, coordination. The bendy body and the ADHD brain don't just live in the same person by coincidence. They're running on the same wiring.
Slide 4 · The clinical lens
In clinic, this changed everything.
When a hypermobile body runs at a higher baseline cost, and the ADHD brain has fewer reliable internal signals to pace, you get the boom-bust cycle. Long stretches of running on adrenaline, then a crash that feels abrupt and total. Heavy limbs, fog, no buffer left.
Slide 5 · The shift
"Push through" stops being the goal.
Pacing is not avoidance. Pacing is learning your current capacity, working within it, and gradually expanding. In a hypermobile body, this is especially relevant — joints rely more on muscle coordination, nervous system regulation, and load sharing than passive ligament support. When activity drops too low, those systems lose practice.
Slide 6 · What it looks like
Steadier exposure beats heroic effort. Every time.
Adjust the amount, the intensity, or the form so the body can keep moving while symptoms stay manageable. Over time, this builds tissue tolerance, coordination, and confidence in movement — the things a sensitive system actually responds to.
